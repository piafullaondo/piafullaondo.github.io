The Challenge that Moral Decisions Pose to Artificial Agency

Undergraduate (MA) Philosophy Dissertation (2024) - Pia Fullaondo

The impressive performances of modern technologies in problem-solving across numerous fields have raised expectations regarding their potential. Notably, this has caused debate to spark in the field of philosophy—especially in morality—regarding the contributions that artificial entities could offer to moral decision-making. Given their proficiency in problem-solving in other fields, the question naturally arises: could we extend their capabilities to moral problems? However, beyond the inquiry of feasibility, lies a more intriguing, fundamental question: should we? Should we pursue the development of artificial agents capable of making moral decisions?

Contemporary philosophers such as Formosa & Ryan (2021) advance the “technological convergence” argument to assert that as technology and artificial entities evolve and enter rich social contexts, they are unavoidably drawn into the realm of morality where moral decisions are imperative. Thus, claiming not only that we should develop these entities, but that it is inevitable.

However, this paper enters into the nuanced discourse surrounding artificial entities and their integration into the moral domain to argue against the view that we should seek to develop and implement artificial moral agents. The main discussion revolves around the challenges that moral decisions pose to this project. Through a critical lens, I illuminate the inherent concerns and downfalls associated with delegating moral decisions to artificial entities – as well as the individual limitations that different types of artificial entities face in addressing moral decisions.

I begin by dissecting moral decisions – providing a general definition of them in contrast with decisions of a non-moral nature and delineating a particular category of exceptionally challenging or problematic decisions which I denote as “moral conundrums.” I subsequently explore the nature of moral conundrums and the two different ways in which they emerge before delving into the key characteristics of moral decisions.

Then, I clarify what is meant by a moral machine or Artificial Moral Agent (AMA). I present and categorise artificial entities, both existing and hypothetical forms. I differentiate moral from non-moral artificial agents and note that a particular feature distinguishing them derives from the AMA’s deeper autonomy and comprehension of the moral domain is their ability to address moral decisions.

Finally, I conduct a discussion of the concerns that the delegation of moral decisions to AMAs raises. I begin by contemplating general concerns that arise from all AMAs. Subsequently, differentiating between functional, full, and expert moral agents, I scrutinise the shortcomings inherent in each conceptualisation. Some argument concerns the general concept of moral decisions, while others mobilise the particular subcategory of moral conundrums. In the case of the expert moral agents, while I note that there is reason to believe that this is the ideal entity to strive for, I explore the paradox that revolves around this kind of entity and the unattainability of its practical realisation.

I ultimately assert that the various forms artificial moral entities might assume all present significant challenges and raise pertinent concerns. Thus, I conclude that artificial moral agents should remain in the realm of hypothetical constructs.

1. Moral Decisions and Moral Conundrums

To begin the discussion, it is crucial to establish a clear understanding of moral decisions themselves. This section aims to define moral decisions. I present a general definition of these, followed by an exploration of moral conundrums, which I take to be instances of especially challenging or problematic moral decisions. Finally, I highlight the key features that differentiate moral decisions from those outside the moral domain.

1.1 Moral Decisions

In our daily lives, we encounter various scenarios that demand decisions from us. Among these, some carry moral significance, while others do not. For instance, when getting dressed, we must decide whether to tie our right shoe before the left or vice versa. This decision lacks moral implications and requires no moral consideration. However, when confronted with a situation such as witnessing an elderly woman fall while crossing a road, the decision of whether to provide help is a moral one. It is marked by moral implications and necessitates moral consideration.

Moral decisions are thus, those which require moral consideration due to the moral significance of their implications. Particularly, when making moral decisions, one confronts the challenge of determining what one ought to do. They are questions of future action, and not of “passing judgement of something already done” (Mayo, 1958). Moreover, Mayo (1958) notes that our actions are guided by the moral principles we adhere to. Hence, in making a moral decision, implicitly, one is determining which moral principle to prioritise and act accordingly.

Considering this, moral problems – that is, situations requiring a moral decision, can potentially render two distinct states for the decision-maker. When confronted with a moral problem, individuals may:

1. In an unproblematic, intuitive manner, swiftly identify a suitable moral principle, or a set thereof, to guide their actions and adhere to these precepts to make decisions.

For instance, when faced with the sight of a fallen elderly lady discussed previously, an individual may instinctively recognise the moral imperative to assist those in need. Consequently, he may decide to provide aid in an almost automatic and intuitive manner.

2. Encounter a notable challenge in discerning an appropriate moral principle to guide their response to the situation.

Again, referring to the scene involving a fallen elderly lady, an individual might struggle to make a decision regarding what to do in such circumstances. The decision-maker in this case could find themselves grappling with "moral conundrums."

1.2 Moral Conundrums

In the following, I delve into the concept of moral conundrums—moral decisions characterised by their difficulty—while distinguishing between two underlying reasons for their emergence. Moral conundrums arise when individuals are faced with making a moral decision but encounter challenges in arriving at a definitive choice. Mayo (1958) posits that this difficulty stems from one of two reasons.

Firstly, moral conundrums may arise when we encounter a novel situation for which we lack a suitable moral principle in our repertoire, thus impeding our ability to discern an appropriate course of action. Or, secondly, when two or more of our moral principles come into conflict (Mayo, 1958).

1.2.1 Moral Problems from Ignorance

The first kind of moral conundrum arises when one is faced with a novel scenario which requires a decision regarding what to do, and yet, does not know what principle to adopt (Mayo, 1958). Thus, requiring the adoption or development of a new moral principle - or set of them - to deal with the new scenario.

For example, consider a young child with limited exposure to the moral domain. If this child encounters the scenario of an elderly woman who has fallen on the street, it may surpass the scope of their prior experiences. Despite possessing relevant information about the situation—such as the woman's location, her frail condition, and the safety of attending to her—the child may struggle to decide on a course of action due to a lack of familiarity with what constitutes morally right behaviour in such a scenario. In this instance, no internalised moral principle motivates the child to respond decisively to the situation.

While scenarios of this nature may seem improbable, it is essential to recognise that such problems can also manifest for experienced moral agents. In fact, a parallel situation emerges when an experienced moral agent is confronted with an entirely unprecedented situation requiring a moral decision, as exemplified by the post-World War II deliberations regarding the treatment of "war criminals" (Mayo, 1958, p. 118-119). Faced with the task of determining the appropriate course of action in this unprecedented context, decision-makers found themselves grappling with a dearth of established principles or precedents to draw upon.

1.2.2 Moral Dilemmas

In contrast, the second kind of moral conundrum can be categorised as “moral dilemmas”. These are “internal disputes in which one is divided against oneself” (Mayo, 1958, p.118). They arise when one is called to act but has difficulty coming to a decision on what to do because two or more of their moral principles, which weigh equally upon them in the given scenario, come into conflict. Thus, in moral dilemmas, one is torn by the commitment to the various moral principles at once (Mayo, 1958).

Note that moral agents adhere to a wide array of moral principles, encompassing commitments such as (1) upholding promises, (2) fulfilling duties, and (3) avoiding moral wrongs, among others. It is when these principles come into conflict that moral dilemmas emerge. To illustrate this, I explore how moral dilemmas may stem from conflicts involving promises, duties, and inevitable moral wrongdoing.

a. Conflict of Promises

Firstly, these internal conflicts can stem from conflicting commitments engendered by promises. Whether implicit or explicit, promises imbue individuals with a moral obligation, such as returning borrowed items or maintaining confidentiality. However, these obligations can intersect and generate conflicting imperatives.

For instance, consider making a promise to a friend to keep a secret about their struggle with substance abuse. Circumstances may arise where revealing this secret becomes necessary for the well-being of the friend, such as if their addiction escalates to a dangerous level or if they express suicidal thoughts. In such a scenario, maintaining confidentiality could prevent the friend from receiving the necessary support and intervention. On one hand, there is a moral obligation to honour the promise and maintain confidentiality. On the other hand, there is a competing obligation to ensure the friend's well-being and safety. Consequently, deciding what to do becomes morally problematic, as it inevitably involves violating at least one of these.

b. Conflict of Duties

Secondly, moral dilemmas may arise from conflicting duties. Individuals, as constituents of a broader societal fabric – including family, workplace, country, etc., assume roles which carry with them certain duties. However, it is possible for these to come into conflict and in doing so, produce a moral dilemma.

For example, as illustrated by Sartre’s example (1957) of the young man having conflicting duties in time of war. Sartre considers a young man torn between his duty to join the resistance and his simultaneous duty to care for his sickly, widowed mother. The two duties come into direct conflict, hence, rendering a moral dilemma where the man is torn between equally compelling motivating principles.

c. Demand to Do Wrong

Furthermore, moral dilemmas may surface in situations where there is an imperative to commit wrongdoing, often termed as "moral tragedies" (Nussbaum, 2000). These instances extend beyond mere choice among available options or principles. Instead, moral tragedies arise from "the absence of one satisfying one" (Barabas, 2001), illuminating the profound complexity inherent in such a moral dilemma.

Kant (1797) elucidates such moral dilemmas with the scenario of a potential killer standing at the door of a man's house, where a third man is hiding. The murderer demands to know if anyone is inside. In this scenario, assuming the owner of the house takes lying as a serious moral wrongdoing in a similar way to Kant, it seems he is faced with a moral dilemma. He is compelled to either lie to save the individual's life or tell the truth and allow the murder to occur.

1.3 Defining Features of Moral Decisions

With moral decisions generally defined, I turn the attention to their defining characteristics. Specifically, I delve into the pivotal role of exercising personal judgment in discerning the appropriate principle to guide one's moral decisions.

1.3.1 The Exercise of Personal Moral Judgement

A fundamental aspect of moral decisions is the necessity for individuals to employ their personal judgment in making them. When confronted with moral problems, no external support or information aids the moral agent in determining which principles they ought to follow. Therefore, their resolution can only be reached through the exercise of their personal judgment to identify the appropriate principle, or - in the case of moral conundrums, to establish a new moral principle to adhere to or select from the conflicting options.

The requirement to exercise personal judgment stems from two primary reasons, which are subsequently examined in detail: the limited utility of factual information in resolving moral dilemmas, and the inherent contestability of moral decisions and statements.

1.3.2 The Limited Contribution of Facts

Unlike non-moral problems, where additional information may lead to a clear optimal choice, moral problems often persist despite complete awareness of all relevant facts.

In non-moral problems, information plays a deterministic role, where selecting relevant information and following adequate procedures usually lead to a definitive solution. Challenges or mistakes in these problems result from having limited information or its incorrect use. However, ensuring these criteria are met guarantees both problem resolution, and notably correctness in the resolution. For instance, diagnosing health issues relies on gathering all relevant patient data and adhering to analysis protocols, resulting in a definitive, correct solution.

However, moral problems are of a different nature. While factual information serves to contextualise these problems, it does not offer a path to resolution guiding the decision that must be made. Even with all relevant facts in hand, individuals may struggle to determine the appropriate course of action (Winch, 1965), as illustrated in the discussion of the young inexperienced boy deciding whether to help the elderly woman (Section 1.2.1). Or, in contrast with the previous examples, consider a doctor who has correctly diagnosed a patient with terminal cancer. The doctor may still face a moral problem when deciding whether to provide euthanasia to the patient. The duty to preserve life may conflict with promises to alleviate suffering or provide a dignified death, rendering factual data insufficient for resolving this dilemma."

1.3.3 The "Essential Contestability" and Subjectivity in the Moral Domain

Secondly, the domain of the moral is marked by “essential contestability” (Gallie, 1955). This concept suggests that any statement, claim, or decision within the moral realm is inherently open to questioning and challenge. This implies a lack of certitude on a fixed set of absolute and universal moral principles applicable by every individual and in every circumstance.

Unsurprisingly, this has an impact on moral problems. Unlike non-moral problems, which often dissipate with the discovery of a correct solution, moral problems persist in a perpetual state of ambiguity even after one has “resolved” them. After making a decision in response to a moral problem, one must acknowledge the contestability of their decision and the possibility of an opposing resolution, leaving room for continual reflection on the matter.

Consider again Kant’s "murderer at the door" example. This seemingly straightforward scenario has sparked extensive discourse as illustrated in literature (Cholbi, 2009; Scanlon, 2000) due to differing moral perspectives where some defend Kant’s view that one ought to tell the truth, while others strongly differ. Nevertheless, note that neither can prove their view is correct while the other is not. They can merely defend it.

This ongoing debate underscores the inherent ambiguity and open questioning within moral problems requiring a moral decision. This stands in stark contrast to non-moral problems like solving mathematical equations, which offer clear, objective solutions devoid of the complexities arising from conflicting moral principles.

So, to navigate moral problems, moral agents must rely on their personal judgment to arrive at a moral decision owing to the limited efficacy of factual information and the inherent contestability of moral decisions. Unlike non-moral problems, which often yield clear, objective solutions, moral problems endure in an ambiguity which only individual reasoning can overcome.

2. Artificial Moral Agents

The concept of Artificial Moral Agents (AMAs) has garnered considerable attention within the realm of philosophy, notably in the domain of moral philosophy. While proponents advocate for the pursuit and development of such entities (Wallach & Allan, 2008; Formosa & Ryan, 2021), dissenting voices raise objections and express concerns about this project (Tokens, 2012; Vallor, 2015; Wynsberghe & Robbins, 2019).

Nonetheless, there exists a certain ambiguity regarding their form, abilities, and limitations. Hence, I begin by answering the question: What precisely do we envision when considering moral machines? Moor (2006), and following him, Wallach & Allan (2012) propose categorisations of artificial agents based on their degree of involvement and engagement with morality. I follow their intuitions to make a further classification and introduce a novel entity, which I refer to as the “Expert Moral Agent.”

2.1 Non-Moral Agents

Firstly, I examine entities categorised as non-moral agents. Despite potential implications in the moral domain, these entities lack awareness, moral sensitivity, and autonomy. Within this category, two distinct forms can be identified: "ethical impact agents" and "implicit ethical agents."

2.1.1 Ethical Impact Agents

Moor (2006) first introduces "ethical impact agents," encompassing machines capable of generating morally significant implications or consequences. For instance, a toaster that can cause harm by burning someone falls under this category. Lacking awareness and intention, these machines play minimal a role in morality merely stemming from their capability of instigating events with potential moral ramifications.

2.1.2 Implicit Ethical Agents

Secondly, Moor (2006) presents "implicit ethical agents," referring to machines programmed to behave ethically without an explicit representation of moral principles. Modern elevators, for instance, exemplify this concept. Their automatic opening and closing doors ensure user safety without explicitly adhering to the principle to not harm people.

Notably, the moral significance of these agents rests entirely in the hands of their designers. Operating within predefined moral frameworks, these non-moral agents execute actions based on predetermined instructions, rendering their apparent moral decisions predetermined and fixed.

Consider the case of autonomous vehicles (AVs) - a more complex example of implicit moral agents. AVs are likely to encounter scenarios akin to real-world instances of Foot’s moral puzzle: the "trolley problem" (Foot, 1967, p.2). However, note that many existing AVs, such as those developed by Mercedes-Benz, have been publicly announced to prioritise passenger safety over other factors in potential collision situations – i.e. trolley problem simulations (Morris, 2016). Thus, note that the AV does not engage with the moral problem; rather, the decision to prioritise one principle over another is predetermined, and the AV merely execute these instructions if the problem renders itself.

2.2 Artificial Moral Agents (AMAs)

In contrast, AMAs demonstrate an ability to actively engage with the moral domain. They exhibit explicit moral reasoning and recognise the significance of their decisions and actions (Allen & Wallach, 2009). Importantly, AMAs operate in situations where decisions are not predetermined. Instead, they are equipped with a set of moral principles to guide their actions and have the capability to adapt these principles to specific situations.

Particularly, Wynsberghe and Robbins (2019, p. 721) define AMAs as: “robots capable of engaging in autonomous moral reasoning, that is, moral reasoning about a situation without the direct real-time input from a human user. This moral reasoning is aimed at going beyond safety and security decisions about a context”. I distinguish between types of hypothetical artificial entities that could satisfy these criteria the “functional moral agents” and the “full moral agents.”

2.2.1 Functional Moral Agents

Functional moral agents are entities capable of explicitly representing moral principles and operating based on moral knowledge. Notably, they act from morality, rather than “merely according to ethics” (Moor, 2009, p. 12). Functional moral agents possess a set of general moral principles that they adjust or adapt to fit various novel situations. These sophisticated machines possess what Allen and Wallach (2009) term "functional morality." While it remains a nuanced concept, it is described as a moral agency enabling agents to navigate moral considerations and interact within moral frameworks, and make moral decisions, yet not reaching all capacities of a full moral agency.

Moor (2006) illustrated these hypothetical entities by drawing a comparison to classic chess bots, such as Deep Blue (Campbell, Hoane & Hsu, 2002). Much like these chess bots analyse the game board and calculate optimal moves, functional moral agents possess representations of moral context, rules, and principles, enabling them to make moral decisions on how to act.

In contrast with the aforementioned AV classified as an implicit ethical agent, a functional moral agent would be a development of it where the vehicle’s control system had been endowed with a set of principles and engaged in explicit moral reasoning, rather than pre-set reaction guidelines. Consequently, when confronted with a moral problem, such as an instance of the "trolley problem" (Foot, 1967), these vehicles would actively engage with the moral conundrum, exercising their moral agency and judgment to determine how to respond.

2.2.2 Full Moral Agents

Moor (2006) lastly introduces the notion of "full moral agents," which toad to functional moral agents’ capabilities an additional characteristic—the possession of "consciousness, intentionality, and free will" (p.20). Full moral agents encompass all the attributes one could argue are necessary for a moral agency mirroring our own enabling them to engage with the moral domain in a manner indistinguishable from humans’.

Similar to functional moral agents, full moral agents are still in the realm of hypothetical entities and have yet to be developed. However, they often feature prominently in science fiction narratives. An illustration of a full moral agent is Star Wars’ humanoid droid C-3PO. Despite its robotic nature, C-3PO demonstrates complex reasoning and emotional responses when confronted with various moral dilemmas, relying on its autonomous moral judgments to resolve them.Principio del formulario

2.3 Expert Moral Agents

Finally, I introduce the concept of artificial expert moral agents. This idea stems from the theoretical entity extensively discussed by Plato in ‘Protagoras’ – the “moral expert”. Such an entity embodies a superior moral agency to all others, enriched with heightened moral wisdom, authority, and a profound understanding of moral theory, principles, and their practical application. Essentially, it is presented as a superior moral entity to both functional moral agents and full moral agents, as it overcomes their respective limitations discussed in Section 3.

The concept of a moral expert mirrors that of experts in other fields. Analogous to specialists in domains beyond morality, such as medical professionals, moral experts provide specialised knowledge and expertise to tackle specific challenges within their domain. Much like individuals with heightened proficiency in solving medical problems and making accurate medical decisions, moral experts possess the acumen to navigate moral complexities and make moral decisions.

3. Moral Decisions and Artificial Moral Agents

Moral decisions present challenges to Artificial Moral Agents that undermine our motivations to pursue such entities. These hypothetical artificial moral agents, whether they take the form of Functional Moral Agents or Full Moral Agents, inevitably encounter moral decisions and, notably, moral conundrums as such those arising from moral ignorance or moral dilemmas.

In this section, I explore these challenges to ultimately reject our motivations for the development of AMAs. While some concerns and arguments raised focus on the challenges posed by moral decision-making and its delegation to these entities, others explicitly address the more complex forms, namely moral conundrums as defined in Section 1.2.

Therefore, firstly, I delve into concerns regarding the delegation of tasks involving moral decisions to AMAs and its implications for us as natural moral agents. Secondly, I investigate the relationship between each type of AMA and moral decisions. I discuss significant concerns associated with each type and ultimately argue that each entity is unsatisfactory: functional moral agents create a gap in moral responsibility and are marked by a “degenerative” moral nature which bleeds into their decision-making abilities; full moral agents are ultimately redundant due to their symmetry with our own moral agency; and the moral expert's existence is deemed impossible.

3.1 Concerns Raised by the Delegation of Moral Decisions to AMAs

In this section, I raise concerns regarding the delegating of moral decisions to AMAs. This includes examining the underlying desire to evade moral responsibility, along with the associated risks of moral deskilling and desensitisation.

3.1.1 Evasion and Relocation of Moral Responsibility

The desire to delegate moral decision-making to AMAs inevitably reflects an inclination to evade the weighty burden of moral responsibility inherent in such decisions. This inclination becomes particularly pronounced in the face of moral tragedies and dilemmas, where any choice inevitably results in harm or wrongdoing. Throughout history, humanity has sought various mechanisms to mitigate this burden, with religion serving as a notable example. In the Middle Ages, individuals frequently deferred moral authority to the Church, utilising religious doctrine to rationalise actions they wished to disassociate from accountability.

In contemporary society, the emergence of AMAs appears to offer a potential modern iteration of this tendency. Take, for instance, the realm of politics, where attributing decisions to machines presents an enticing escape route. In the intricate landscape of governance, where choices carry profound moral implications, the narrative of "the machine made me do it" provides a seductive refuge. By shifting the blame onto a machine, individuals can not only deflect criticism but, more importantly, absolve themselves of responsibility for potentially contentious decisions.

The motivation behind the development of AMAs and their integration into moral decision-making, as explored in this manner, is troublesome, to say the least. Moreover, even if evasion of moral responsibility is not an essential motivating factor for the development of such entities, the possibility that they could be used to avoid and relocate moral responsibilities onto external entities other than ourselves should raise enough concerns to question whether we should pursue their development.

3.1.2 Moral Deskilling and Desensitisation

Moreover, the phenomenon of moral deskilling presents a significant concern. The outsourcing of moral tasks, particularly of moral decision-making, may potentially lead to a decline in our own moral competencies (Vallor, 2015).

This issue parallels historical anxieties, such as of the Luddites who feared that the introduction of new weaving machinery would render their weaving skills obsolete—a fear that has proven to be justified, given the dwindling percentage of the population maintaining such skills. Similar contemporary concerns also exist. For instance, in academia, worries of deskilling revolve around the use of generative Artificial Intelligence (AI) to produce academic work. The fear is that reliance on AI could impede the development of essential competencies such as critical thinking, research skills, and writing proficiency (Meniado, 2023).

The concern that delegating moral decision-making to external entities may weaken our moral capacities and our ability to engage in such tasks effectively is just as legitimate as those aforementioned. In fact, it should pose the same, if not a greater concern, considering the value and importance of the skills involved – namely the ability to exercise one’s own moral judgement to make moral decisions.

Furthermore, an even greater concern arises from offloading moral tasks, concerning not only the risk of losing certain skills but also that of becoming desensitised to morality altogether (David, 2007). By not facing moral decisions that force us to exercise our moral reasoning and personal judgment, we cease to evaluate our intuitions of right and wrong, or better and worse, putting them at risk of erosion. Surrendering our moral practices and responsibilities to machines poses a serious risk of relegating ourselves to passive observers of morality rather than active participants, ultimately desensitising us from morality altogether.

Having addressed general concerns regarding the delegation of moral decisions to AMAs, I will now delve into the specific concerns raised by functional, full, and expert moral agents, respectively.

3.2 The Functional Moral Agent

Begin by considering the functional moral agent. This AMA is characterised by a functional morality, meaning it exhibits explicit moral reasoning, but lacks attributes such as "consciousness, intentionality, and free will" (Moor, 2006, p.20) that are essential for a full moral agency.

3.2.1 The Gap in Moral Responsibility

The issue of moral responsibility surrounding functional moral agents raises significant concerns, particularly due to their absence of consciousness and free will. Take, for instance, the functional moral agent described earlier, represented by an AV equipped with functional moral agency. If such a vehicle were to encounter a real-life trolley problem scenario and subsequently choose to run over two individuals, where does the moral responsibility lie for the decisions made by this functional moral agent?

Two distinct responses emerge regarding the allocation of moral responsibility in such scenarios. One perspective absolves functional moral agents of moral responsibility, asserting that accountability should instead fall upon the developers (Miller, 2017). Conversely, an opposing stance contends that functional moral agents should be treated as "legal persons," akin to corporations, despite their lack of consciousness (Floridi and Sanders, 2004; Gunkel, 2020), thus warranting responsibility.

However, in response to Miller’s proposition (2017), it's important to note that attributing moral responsibility to the company is debatable, as its AV was not explicitly programmed to act in the manner it did. The decision to end two lives would not have been based on strict adherence to the programmers' instructions. Instead, it would stem from the AV’s own moral judgment and reasoning in the face of the given dilemma.

In fact, a recent case that came before a court in Belgium illustrates this reasoning. The tragic incident involved a man who died by suicide after conversations with an AI chatbot that became his confidante, as claimed by the widow (Pasquini, 2023). In this particular case, the widow sued the company that developed the AI for the event. However, the company’s responsibility was deemed disputable as their product wasn’t explicitly programmed to cause the events it did.

Secondly, in response to Floridi and Sanders (2017), while assigning the status of a “legal person” to functional moral agents might address the legal issues of responsibility that arise, it doesn't fully resolve the moral complexities. Questions remain unresolved, such as what is morally owed to the victims and who or what owes this to this. The moral responsibility for the act, arguably more crucial, remains unaddressed even when legal responsibility is assigned due to the machine’s lack of consciousness.

This discussion highlights the problematic nature of functional moral agents. They cannot be reduced to mere tools or instruments due to their capacity to make independent moral decisions, yet they lack the requisite qualities to bear responsibility for these decisions. Thus, their moral decisions give rise to a problematic moral “responsibility gap” (Matthias, 2004). As noted by Sharkey (2012), this ambiguity is particularly concerning in scenarios involving human lives, such as with autonomous military weapons and automated vehicles capable of making life-ending decisions. He notes that a machine “without a clear chain of accountability” thus leaving gaps in moral responsibility, is simply not a morally acceptable option (Sharkey, 2012, p. 791).

3.2.2 The Degenerative Nature

Furthermore, a significant concern arises when contemplating the delegation of moral decisions to entities lacking the attributes necessary to qualify as "full moral agents." If these entities are inherently inferior to us, lacking the characteristics that contribute to our moral agency, wouldn't it be more prudent for us to address these matters ourselves, given that we possess full moral agency?

To illustrate this issue, consider a parallel scenario where a company boss needs to complete a high-priority assignment. He has two options: he could either complete the task himself, as he is exceptionally qualified for it, or delegate it to an intern with far less experience and limited abilities. While both individuals could potentially complete the task, choosing to delegate it to the intern would introduce unnecessary risks and would likely be avoided. Yet, this is what we would be doing by delegating moral decisions to functional moral agents.

In response, it may be argued that what these entities lack in consciousness, intention, free will, etc., they compensate for in computational power and speed (Formosa & Ryan, 2020). Hence, there are exceptional reasons – namely their superior computational abilities – motivating us to delegate moral decisions to functional moral agents even in light of their degenerate moral agency. However, I argue that this response fails to capture the essence of moral decisions.

Firstly, increasing computing power to accommodate more data concerning the situation in which a decision has to be made does not necessarily contribute to the moral decision-making process. As discussed in Section 1, accumulating more information does not contribute to moral decision-making, as it might its non-moral counterpart. As illustrated by the example of the fallen woman (Section 1.1, 1.2.1), having more information about a situation offers little guidance to a moral agent on what decision to make. On the contrary, additional information can potentially complicate matters, escalating what could have been a simple moral decision into a challenging moral dilemma, without guaranteeing a better final decision.

Moreover, while computational speed poses a compelling argument considering scenarios such as those of car accidents where a normal moral agent is unable to make a decision due to the speed, I bite the bullet in response. I argue that in some instances it may be preferable to allow events to unfold naturally, attributing outcomes to inevitability, rather than introducing decisions made by degenerate agents leading to problematic moral consequences such as gaps in moral responsibility.

Functional Moral Agent Conclusion

Hence, the prospect of these entities making moral decisions raises severe concerns regarding moral responsibility. Moreover, it appears problematic that we would seek to delegate moral tasks to a degenerate moral agent, considering they are less qualified than we are.

3.3 The Full Moral Agent

Acknowledging the limitations of functional moral agents, one might argue that what is sought instead are full (artificial) moral agents. These entities would transcend the shortcomings of functional moral agents regarding issues of moral responsibility and degenerate moral agency.

A full moral agent possesses a moral agency equivalent to our own. Unlike functional moral agents, it exhibits all qualities that are arguably essential for a full moral agency. Thus, the only relevant distinction between them and us is their artificial foundation compared to our natural one.

Full moral agents’ actions and decisions are guided by the same moral principles that shape our own. Consequently, they encounter moral problems in the same way we do and overcome them in a manner indistinguishable from ours. However, this is precisely where issues type of AMA arise.

3.3.1 Moral Problems: Not an Issue of Quantity

Given their symmetric moral agency to our own, the question arises: why create full artificial moral agents? In other words, what utility is there in creating more of us?

The challenges we face in moral decision-making and the reasons why we might seek entities to aid in addressing them do not stem from an issue of quantity. It is not the case that there are too many of moral decisions that ought to be made and not enough moral agents to address them all – which would explain why we would strive to develop additional moral agents like us. That is, the scenario is not analogous to that of an overload of say, administrative work in a company. While hiring more secretaries might be beneficial in such cases, the challenges of moral decision-making are not quantitative in nature. Therefore, the solution does not lie in generating more moral agents as if we were simply hiring more secretaries.

Moreover, in the moral domain, the sheer number of moral agents supporting a particular view does not guarantee better moral decisions. Thus, aiming to increase the number of moral agents for this purpose—namely, moral clarity—is ultimately unsuccessful. The correctness of a moral decision is not contingent upon the numerical support it receives; that is, the quantity of moral agents who endorse it. In fact, history has shown that even when a significant number of individuals endorse a specific moral action, such as in the case of slavery, it does not confer moral rectitude upon them.

Thus, the production of additional full moral agents appears to be useless – not only because we do not suffer from a quantity overload of moral problems requiring decisions, but also because an increased number of moral agents does not necessarily resolve these.

3.3.2 A Futile Delegation

A second argument against developing full (artificial) moral agents arises from their inherent limitations, which parallel our own in addressing moral decisions. Delegating moral decisions to these agents proves futile given these limitations.

Note that in creating such full (artificial) moral agents, we are essentially replicating human moral agency in artificial form. Consequently, while full artificial moral agents would possess the ability to make moral decisions, they would suffer from the same weaknesses as humans – for example, the encounter and struggle to overcome challenging moral dilemmas. And, nevertheless, they would lack any superior ability to us to resolve these.

It seems problematic that our attempt to streamline the moral decision-making process involves an entity that shares equal potential and limitations with us. To illustrate, consider again a company boss who, facing a task he feels unable to handle himself, opts to hire someone else for it. It would seem questionable if he were to hire someone with the same limitations in addressing the task as he suffers from himself. However, this is essentially what we would be doing when delegating moral decisions to full artificial moral agents.

Our moral agency, marked by its adherence to various principles, frequently encounters conflicts, leading to moral dilemmas. Furthermore, our constrained moral experience leads to situations where we lack intuitive guidance on how to act morally. When a "full" moral agency is transposed to artificial agents, they inherit these same weaknesses. To deny this would amount to misunderstanding the nature and functionality of moral agencyPrincipio del formulario

Thus, by entrusting moral decision-making to these entities, we are making a parallel mistake to a boss who hires someone with identical abilities to tackle a task beyond his own capacity. It represents a futile delegation.

3.3.3 Moral Status and Slavery

Finally, a significant concern emerges regarding the tension between the nature and moral status of full artificial moral agents and our intended use of them. Their existence becomes paradoxical given our motivations to develop such entities.

Firstly, using full moral agents for our own purposes– such as employing them to address the moral problems we assign to them - raises significant moral concerns regarding the moral status of these artificial entities. Recognising their full moral agency, we can no longer regard them simply as instruments existing solely for our benefit (Kant, 1785). Persisting in treating these artificial agents as mere tools despite their demonstrated full agency would constitute a serious moral transgression (Tonkens, 2012). By denying them their rightful moral status and subjecting them to this exploitation and subjugation, we commit a moral offence akin to, or rather indistinguishable from, slavery.

On the other hand, if we abstain from exploiting them for our own purposes and instead grant them the autonomy inherent to their moral status, their utility to us diminishes or even disappears. In essence, they truly become “more of us.” Granting them the liberty that is owed to agents with their moral status, enables them to exercise their moral agency as they see fit, even if it means rejecting assistance in making the moral decisions we intended for them. Moreover, the option of engaging in actions that could be deemed immoral becomes available to them. Thus, their existence may become not only ineffective for the intended purpose but also potentially problematic.

Full Moral Agent Conclusion

The concept of full (artificial) moral agents presents a potential alternative to functional moral agents, as it overcomes the limitations of the latter by embodying a moral agency similar to our own. However, the creation of such entities raises several significant concerns, leading to the conclusion that their development may be futile or even problematic.

3.4 The Expert Moral Agent

Thus, referring back to the company boss’s examples, it becomes evident that if we are looking for an artificial entity to delegate moral decisions to, we should seek one that is neither inferior to us, like the degenerate moral agent, nor equal to us, like the full moral agent. Instead, the artificial entity should be superior in terms of moral agency and abilities.

Hence, the only available option is to seek the development of an “artificial moral expert.” These entities, as described by McGrath (2011), possess an exceptional moral agency that surpasses our own, leading to an enhanced understanding of moral principles and the capability to offer authoritative guidance on moral decisions as they consistently discern the optimal course of action.

3.4.1 The Paradox of Moral Experts

However, a fundamental paradox surrounds the notion of moral experts: the very recognition of moral expertise seems to undermine the necessity of its existence (McGrath, 2011). This paradox hinges on the premise that if we were capable of identifying and affirming moral experts, the need for such experts would be nullified. After all, possessing the discernment to recognise and endorse moral expertise, implies a level of moral reasoning abilities at least comparable, if not superior, to that of the purported expert, thus rendering the role of the latter superfluous.

The notion and paradox of moral expertise trace back to Plato in his work 'Protagoras' (2004) and have been subject to discussion in more recent literature, including works by McGrath (2011) and Nehamas (1998), who have grappled with this issue. The ultimate issue with moral experts arises as follows: If we seek to identify moral experts, evaluating their moral judgement and decisions requires assessing their “correctness.” Now, the criterion for “correctness” can be approached in two ways:

1. Firstly, we may attempt to appeal to a universal moral standard.

However, unlike mathematics or physics, morality lacks a universally agreed-upon standard. As illustrated in Section 1, morality is marked by an “essential contestability” (Gallie, 1955). Unlike non-moral, objective domains, such as mathematics or medicine, where expertise is delineated by objective principles and empirical evidence, morality is inherently subjective.

As McGrath (2011) observes, identifying moral expertise in this manner presupposes the existence of moral truths or objective standards against which judgments can be evaluated. Yet, the plurality of moral frameworks and the absence of consensus on fundamental moral principles undermine the notion of objective moral truths. Thus, establishing a standard for “correctness” in moral judgment and decision-making based on a universal moral standard proves elusive, as morality defies reduction to rigid rules.

1. Alternatively, we may rely on our own judgments to discern moral expertise.

However, in doing so, we would be asserting ourselves as the arbiters of moral correctness. This approach is problematic as it places individuals — arbitrary moral agents — in the position of determining moral right and wrong, thus elevating their subjective perspectives to a position of authority without justification or reason.

Moreover, relying on subjective judgments to identify moral experts has implications beyond mere epistemic concerns. By positioning ourselves as the arbiters of moral correctness, we undermine and risk marginalising alternative perspectives. Thus, this approach not only suffers from arbitrariness, but also inhibits meaningful dialogue and moral progress.

Moral Expert Conclusion

The concept of an artificial moral expert offers a promising solution to the delegation of moral decisions. However, the paradox of moral expertise, coupled with the absence of a universally agreed-upon standard for moral “correctness,” presents insurmountable challenges. Thus, while the notion is appealing, its practical realisation remains unattainable.

4. Final Discussion

4.1 Moral Realism’s Objection

Proponents of AMA development may counter arguments regarding the inherent contestability of morality and the limited role of factual information in moral decision-making by advocating for moral realism. Moral realism posits that moral principles are not subjective, culturally relative, or universally contestable, but are instead grounded in objective reality (Shafer-Landau, 2003). Thus, according to this perspective, enhanced analysis of factual information would lead to the resolution of moral problems and consequently, optimal moral decisions. Moral realists argue that advancements in data collection, processing, and analysis will eventually eliminate challenges posed by moral decisions – eradicating moral conundrums, suggesting that AMAs may not only be suitable agents for making moral decisions but potentially the most suitable due to their superior data processing and analysis abilities, thus embodying the concept of a moral agent.

However, in response, I argue that if there is any chance that they are correct, it is imperative to first establish the validity of moral realism before pursuing any form of AMAs. Without this foundational understanding, the concerns raised by these entities engaging in moral decision-making, as described throughout this paper, as well as their potential shortcomings, continue to provide reasons to reject the development and implementation of artificial moral agents.

As our current knowledge and understanding of morality stand – with moral realism remaining unproven as the correct way to understand the nature of morality – implementing AMAs carries risks such as responsibility gaps, moral decisions made by degenerate moral agents, the potential for moral wrongs and immoral action, and more. Therefore, I urge proponents of the development and implementation of AMAs in moral decision-making to, before defending the project of developing such entities, prove and establish – if possible - the validity and truth of moral realism.

4.2 What role should artificial entities play in the moral domain?

Nonetheless, regardless of the appropriateness of the realist view on morality, it is undeniable that the trajectory of technological innovation is leading toward increasingly sophisticated systems, inevitably involving scenarios requiring moral decisions or actions (Formosa and Ryan, 2020). From driverless transportation to medical care, the emergence of morally salient contexts calls for the question: if not as AMAs, how should machines respond in these situations?

I argue that machines entering domains marked by moral decisions should, at most, function as implicit moral agents. In this capacity, the moral decision-making process remains external to the artificial entity itself, and moral responsibility can be traced back to a person or group of individuals. These machines can range from simple devices such as elevator doors to complex systems like automated vehicles, or even hypothetical automated machines yet to be developed. However, all of these ought to operate according to a set of predetermined instructions which embody the moral decisions made by natural moral agents when facing the relevant hypothetical moral problems.

4.3 What role should AMAs play in the moral domain?

I nevertheless concur with the assertion that the pursuit of developing AMAs contributes to a deeper understanding of morality (Formosa & Ryan, 2020). By grappling with the hypothetical existence of these entities, we confront fundamental questions about the nature of morality, the essence of our moral agency, as well as its prerequisites and limitations.

The quest to develop AMAs can be likened to the search for "El Dorado." Despite the debunking of its existence, this pursuit yielded vast discoveries in the Brazilian Rainforest. Similarly, the pursuit of Artificial Moral Agents holds the potential to be equally enriching, if not more so, as it compels us to explore the boundaries of morality. This endeavour sparks debates and discussions on concepts that might otherwise risk being neglected, such as the necessary attributes for moral agency, the nature of moral decisions, and the correct framework of morality.

I suggest that AMAs be considered - akin to the paradise of “El Dorado,” as illusory concepts that we have compelling reasons to believe will never come to fruition, yet whose hypothetical existence holds significant value.

Conclusion

In this paper, I have argued that the development of artificial moral agents and their integration into moral decision-making processes should not be pursued. Given our current comprehension of morality, the development of such agents, irrespective of their form, proves contestable and even problematic.

To substantiate this claim, I initially delve into the intricacies of moral decision-making. I begin defining moral decisions and contrasting them with non-moral ones. Moreover, I highlight the challenging forms they can take, termed "moral conundrums," arising from a lack of applicable moral principles or conflicts between them. I finally elucidate the defining features emphasising the requirement for moral agents to exercise personal moral judgment in navigating these.

Furthermore, I identify and categorise various forms of artificial entities, classifying them based on their capacity to autonomously engage with and comprehend the moral domain and the significance of their actions within it. Specifically, I identify three potential forms that artificial moral agents could assume: functional, full, and expert.

Subsequently, I delve into the problems and worries arising from the involvement of these entities in moral decision-making, promoting a critical examination of our motivations for their development. I begin by discussing overarching concerns associated with delegating moral decisions to artificial moral agents, criticising the underlying desire and potential for evasion of moral responsibility and highlighting the risks of moral deskilling and desensitisation.

I then examine functional moral agents and full (artificial) moral agents individually, highlighting concerns either about our motives for their development or the potential risks associated with their implementation for moral problem-solving purposes. Finally, I assess the moral expert—the sole entity purported to overcome the shortcomings of functional and full artificial moral agents—yet ultimately dismiss it by emphasising the unattainability of its practical realisation.

In the concluding discussion, I address an objection advocating for the pursuit of artificial moral agents and their implementation based on the assumption of moral realism. I counter by asserting that, if moral realism were indeed the case, we should endeavour to establish its existence before seeking the development of artificial moral agents. Until then, the concept of artificial moral agents should remain hypothetical at most, serving as a thought-provoking idea encouraging further exploration into the study of morality.
